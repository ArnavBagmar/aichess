AI Chessathon 2026 · engineering write-up

Gators: an NNUE chess engine in pure Python, and what training six networks taught me

300,000 nodes a second on one core, six trained evaluation networks, a dozen gated search changes, and the measurement discipline that separated the ideas that worked from the ones that only looked good.

Arnav Bagmar September 2026 Source on GitHub
Abstract

AI Chessathon asks for a single agent.py that plays chess on one CPU core with 2 GB of memory, no network, no GPU, and no existing engine anywhere in the zip. I built an engine for it in the Stockfish shape, from scratch, in Python: a HalfKAv2_hm NNUE evaluation trained on my own GTX 1660 with the open nnue-pytorch trainer, integer inference that reproduces the trainer bit-for-bit, and an alpha-beta search compiled by numba over bitboards. The search went from about 22,000 nodes a second on python-chess to roughly 300,000 on hand-written bitboard kernels, and completes depth 13 to 17 in a 3.7 second move where it used to complete 4 to 7. Six networks were trained on 53 billion position samples in total; only two of them were worth shipping, and the reason the others were not is the most useful thing in this write-up. Every search change was accepted or rejected by a self-play sequential probability ratio test, the way Stockfish's own fishtest works, and that discipline caught one change that would have cost 141 Elo. The engine scored 90% in its first ten rated games on the platform.

1The problem the competition sets

The contract is small. The platform imports agent.py, calls get_move(fen, time_left_ms), and expects a UCI move back. The process lives for one game. Import time gets its own 60 second budget, after which the clock runs: 120 seconds per side plus half a second a move, measured on wall time. An illegal move, a crash, running out of memory, or a flag loses the game outright.

The constraints are what make it an engineering problem rather than a chess one:

  • One core, 2 GB, no GPU, no network. Everything the engine needs ships in a zip under 50 MB.
  • No engines. Stockfish, Lc0, Maia, or anything embedding them is an instant disqualification, checked after the fact. Training on engine-annotated data is explicitly allowed; the ban is on what runs inside the zip.
  • No native binaries. Python source only. Model weights are fine. Cython does not work; numba does, but every kernel compiles at import, inside the 60 seconds.
  • A fixed environment. torch 2.13 (CPU), numpy 2.5, python-chess 1.11, onnxruntime 1.29, numba 0.67, and nothing else.
  • Rated games start from curated openings, not the standard position, so an opening book is worth little.

The starter kit came with a legal random mover and a local harness that mirrors the platform's protocol and clock. Everything from there was mine to build, and the question I set myself was: how much of the Stockfish design can be rebuilt in Python, under these limits, in a week and a half?

2Learning from Stockfish without shipping it

Stockfish is open source, and its repository and the companion trainer are the best textbook on modern engine design that exists. I read both closely, and the rules draw a line I kept to: study the ideas, re-implement them from the published descriptions on the Chess Programming Wiki, and never paste source. Pasting would be a rules violation and a GPL problem in one.

What I took from the Stockfish repository and from nnue-pytorch:

  • The evaluation architecture. HalfKAv2_hm features, king buckets, layer-stack buckets by piece count, and the exact integer quantisation pipeline the engine runs at inference. The trainer's quantize.py and config.py are the specification; my nnue_arch.py is a transcription of those constants with a comment per line saying which unit each layer produces.
  • The search toolkit. Principal variation search, aspiration windows, null-move pruning with depth-adaptive reduction, late move reductions on a logarithmic table, late move pruning, reverse futility, static exchange evaluation, singular extensions with multi-cut, history with gravity and malus, an improving flag, internal iterative reductions. Each is a published idea. What Stockfish taught me is not the idea but the sizing: every margin is a number tuned by games, and copying a margin across without re-tuning it is how I lost 141 Elo once (section 5).
  • The training recipe. The master-net commits document their flags: a lambda schedule that blends game results into the target late in training, early-fen skipping, a low learning rate, resuming from the previous net. I tried all of them (section 4). They did less than I expected, and finding out why was the point.
  • The method. Stockfish accepts nothing without a self-play SPRT on fishtest. I built a small fishtest of my own and every search commit went through it. That turned out to be the single most valuable thing I copied.

Stockfish itself never ran inside the submission. It lived outside the repository as a sparring partner for the benchmark, and the packaging script only ever picks up root-level Python and the weights directory, so it could not reach the zip by accident.

3The evaluation: an NNUE in integers

An NNUE is a small fully connected network designed to be updated incrementally as pieces move. The first layer is enormous and sparse; everything after it is tiny. The network I ship is the nnue-pytorch master architecture restricted to the HalfKAv2_hm feature set and sized down to a 256-wide first layer.

LayerShapeWeightsUnits after quantisation
Feature transformer24,576 × 256int161/256 pawn; accumulated per perspective
PSQT columns24,576 × 8int321/9600 pawn, one column per piece-count bucket
Pairwise clipped product256 → 128 per side—0…127, shift by 9
Layer 1 (8 buckets)256 × 32int8squared and linear paths, both clipped to 0…127
Layer 2 (8 buckets)64 × 32int8same shape of activation
Output (8 buckets)128 × 1int81/16384, then scaled to 1/32 centipawn

The feature index is king_bucket × 768 + piece_type × 64 + square: 32 king buckets (the board is mirrored so the king is always on the left half) times 12 piece planes times 64 squares. A position activates at most 32 features per perspective, so evaluating a position from scratch is 32 row-adds of 256 int16 values, and evaluating it after a move is two to four row-adds and subtracts, unless the king moved, in which case that side's accumulator is rebuilt.

The rest of the forward pass is about ten thousand integer multiply-adds: 256 × 32 for the first hidden layer, 64 × 32 for the second, 128 for the output. Measured after the int32 rewrite, a full evaluation costs 1.6 microseconds on a Ryzen 5 3600. Move generation, make-move and the accumulator update take most of the remaining 2 microseconds of a node.

Two things about the integer pipeline mattered more than I expected:

  • Bit-exactness is checkable, so check it. The exporter reads a Lightning checkpoint, merges the trainer's factorised feature weights, applies the quantisation scales and writes an .npz. A separate script then runs the trainer's own float forward pass (with quantisation epsilon disabled) on 300 random positions and compares against my integer kernels. Every shipped net passed at a maximum difference of 0.00 centipawns.
  • Accumulate in int32, not int64. The first version summed the hidden layers in int64. Numba could not vectorise it, and evaluation cost 3.8 of a node's 5.7 microseconds. Switching the sums to int32 let it use SIMD and dropped evaluation to 1.6 µs, taking the whole search from 176 to 236 thousand nodes a second. Same answers, bit for bit, a third more search.

Scores inside the search are integers in 1/32 of a centipawn, positive for the side to move, with mate at one million. Keeping the net's native unit rather than rounding to centipawns costs nothing and means every pruning margin is expressed on the same scale the net was trained on.

4Training six networks on one consumer GPU

All training used the open nnue-pytorch trainer, unmodified, with its C++ data loader built under MinGW because its CMake file passes flags MSVC does not understand. The data is public: the test80 and test77 binpacks published by the Stockfish community on Hugging Face, which are positions from Stockfish self-play labelled with Stockfish's own search score and the game result. Eight shards, 99 GB decompressed, on a spare drive. Training on engine-labelled data is explicitly allowed; what ships is the network I trained on it.

What the six runs actually taught me

  • More epochs on the same data lowers loss and lowers strength. Epoch 150 of net1 beat epoch 108 on the loss curve and lost to it by 30 Elo over 200 games. The net was fitting the two shards it had seen, not chess. This is the cleanest demonstration I have that the loss is a proxy and games are the measurement.
  • Data breadth beat everything else. Going from two shards to six (net2) was the only training change that produced a clear gain. The recipe flags from the Stockfish master nets (net4) and a wider first layer (net3) did not move the rating on this data. Stockfish's own recipe trains on 42 shards, 700 GB, relabelled with a stronger evaluator; the recipe is designed for that regime and my runs were data-starved by two orders of magnitude.
  • A wider net is not free at this speed. net3's 512-wide feature transformer costs a third of the nodes (168 versus 251 thousand a second), and the better evaluation exactly paid for the lost depth: −3 Elo, inside the noise. The lever that would have made it worth it, more and better data, was the one I did not have time for.
  • The failure the nets share is not about the schedule. Both net2 and net5 misjudge king-and-pawn endings by a full rook (a position Stockfish scores at −14.5 pawns, they score at −3) and they under-value promotion when a pawn on the seventh rank is already "worth" a queen to them. Diagnosed from the rated games (section 8), reproduced locally, unchanged by net4 and net5. The fix is training data with those positions in it, deep-labelled, not another pass over test80.
  • Scale the learning-rate schedule to the run you can afford. The trainer's default gamma is sized for 400 epochs; stopping a 400-epoch schedule at 150 leaves the learning rate at 10% of initial instead of 4%. I set gamma = 0.04^(1/epochs) per run, which is the kind of thing the recipe assumes you know.

5The search: from python-chess to numba bitboards

The engine went through three search implementations, each measured before the next one was started.

Phase one: python-chess and a dict

The first search was negamax with alpha-beta over python-chess boards, iterative deepening, a transposition table keyed on the board's transposition key, MVV-LVA plus killers plus history for ordering, quiescence, and a budget derived from the clock. It reached about 22,000 nodes a second and depth 4 to 7 at a tournament budget. A profile of one four-second middlegame search was unambiguous: hundreds of thousands of calls into python-chess for move generation and make-move, 38% of the time in the accumulator-update glue, and the evaluation itself at 4%. Evaluation was nearly free; the board was not.

Phase two: the pruning set, one commit at a time

Before rewriting the board I landed the standard pruning techniques on the slow search, because each buys effective plies without buying nodes: PVS, aspiration windows, check extensions, reverse futility, delta pruning in quiescence, static exchange evaluation for capture ordering and quiescence, a fix for mate scores in the table (they must be stored relative to the node and converted on probe), and a clock check every 256 nodes. Staged move generation was measured speed-neutral and dropped. Each change was one commit gated by self-play against a worktree of the previous commit.

Phase three: everything inside numba

The rewrite replaced python-chess inside the search with numba nopython kernels: bitboard representation with copy-make and Zobrist keys (bitboard.py, 712 lines), accumulators and the integer forward pass over those bitboards (nnue_bitboard.py), and one recursive alpha-beta kernel with quiescence as its depth ≤ 0 branch (search_kernel.py, 838 lines). python-chess is used only at the root, to parse the FEN and to confirm the kernel's move is legal before it is returned. The Python root (search.py) owns every array the kernel touches: the 220-entry transposition table, killers, history, the move stacks, the game's position keys.

The port was deliberately algorithm-preserving, so the first gate measured speed alone: 184 to 303 thousand nodes a second against 13 to 22, ten to fourteen times faster, and +512 Elo in self-play against the python-chess build (18 wins, 2 draws, no losses at 10 s + 0.5 s). Perft and a 200-game differential test agreed with python-chess move for move, and the evaluation was bit-exact with the old engine.

Three things about numba were worth learning the hard way:

  • It compiles a separate specialisation of a kernel for every distinct literal argument at a call site. Calling the search kernel with True at one site and False at another compiled it three times, 31 seconds of the 60-second import budget. The fix was to derive every flag from a value and keep null-move permission in a per-ply array. Import on the platform's core is now about 20 seconds.
  • Reading the clock from inside a kernel costs 0.8 µs via objmode, invisible when checked every 4,096 nodes, and it keeps the time control in the kernel rather than forcing a return to Python.
  • Self-recursion works. numba 0.67 resolves a recursive @njit function from its non-recursive base case, so quiescence can be a branch of the same kernel rather than a second function.

Phase four: retuning for the new depths

A search that reaches depth 9 needs different margins from one that reaches depth 5. This phase added, in gated order: soft and hard time budgets with an instability extension; a move-number horizon so the middlegame gets more time than a fixed divisor gives it; log-shaped LMR, late move pruning, frontier futility; a fifty-move fade and a mop-up term around the net's score so won endings get converted; bounded check extensions (only when SEE ≥ 0 and inside twice the root depth) and a depth-adaptive null-move reduction; then a second wave of an improving flag, bounded history with gravity and malus feeding into LMR, singular extensions with multi-cut, and the int32 forward pass.

The rejected row is the lesson. The improving flag reduces the reverse-futility margin by one ply when the position is getting better, exactly as Stockfish does. At depth 1 that took my margin to zero, so the engine pruned every quiet move at the frontier whenever it thought it was improving, and Stockfish's move-count limits, sized for its move ordering, were too tight for mine. Half a ply of margin and a looser move-count formula turned −141 into part of a +116 wave. The technique was right; the numbers were somebody else's.

6The node arithmetic

This is the part of the project I would put on a whiteboard first. Chess has about 35 legal moves in a typical position. A plain minimax search to depth d visits roughly 35d leaves; alpha-beta with perfect move ordering visits about 35d/2. The whole engineering of a chess engine is about closing the gap between those two numbers and then buying more depth with the nodes that remain.

minimax: nodes ≈ b^d (b ≈ 35) alpha-beta: nodes ≈ b^(d/2) with perfect ordering measured: EBF = nodes^(1/d) the effective branching factor

The engine's budget on the platform is set by the clock. With 120 seconds plus half a second a move and a planning horizon of 40 moves that shrinks with the move number, it spends about 3.7 seconds per move through move 30, which at 300,000 nodes a second is about a million nodes. That budget is where the depth comes from.

The engine completes depth 13 to 17 in a 3.7 second move. Reading the table, that is far past what alpha-beta alone allows: null-move pruning, late move reductions, futility and the rest are what make the measured effective branching factor land well under the square root of 35. The live demo shows this per move: the ratio of nodes between one completed depth and the next is the branching factor the search actually experienced.

Depth 14 for 668 thousand nodes is an effective branching factor of 2.6, against 35 for the raw tree and 5.9 for ideal alpha-beta. That number, more than any single technique, is the summary of the search work: each pruning idea in section 5 lowered it, and the gate measured whether lowering it that particular way was worth the positions it stopped looking at.

Two more numbers frame the node budget. The transposition table is 220 entries of a 64-bit key and a 64-bit packed record (score in the high 32 bits; move, depth, bound and generation below), 16 MB, well inside the 2 GB. And a mate score is one million minus the distance in plies, stored relative to the node and converted on probe, so that a mate found at ply 6 from one root is still the shortest mate when it is read back at ply 2 from the next.

7Measuring honestly

The first rating I measured was wrong, and the way it was wrong shaped the rest of the project. Stockfish's UCI_LimitStrength caps its search rather than scaling with the clock, so at 120 s + 0.5 s the engine scored 75 to 81% against Stockfish set to 1900, 2200 and 2500 alike. Three settings 600 Elo apart cannot all be beaten at the same rate; the dial is calibrated for fast games. I stopped quoting an Elo from it.

What replaced it:

  • Full-strength Stockfish capped by nodes per move (1k, 4k, 16k, 64k), which is monotonic and does not collapse at long time controls, as a yardstick for large changes.
  • Self-play SPRT for every search change. A worktree per commit, the new build against the previous one, alternating colours, 10 s + 0.5 s, the sequential probability ratio test on the hypotheses [0, 20] Elo. The bench plays through the harness's real referee, so the clock, flag-fall and illegal-move rules are the platform's. I calibrated it first: HEAD against HEAD stayed undecided over 100 games, as it must.
  • Physical cores only. The referee measures wall time, so with 6 physical cores I never ran more than 3 games at once, and I verified the per-pair slowdown at 3 workers was 7%. Oversubscribing the box makes the agent look weaker, and the one time three workers ran alongside the GPU trainer's loader processes the machine ran out of memory and every game became a void that the bench tallied as a draw. A run of all draws means init failures, not equal play.

The node-capped ladder still could not separate builds near the top: at 10 s + 0.5 s three different builds all scored 55 to 58% against Stockfish at its 3000 setting. Self-play SPRT is the measurement that resolves 30 Elo; nothing else I had does.

8What the rated games showed

Two builds played on the platform under two names. "Something" was the first bitboard build with the epoch-108 net; "Gators" was the retuned search with net2. I annotated 42 rated games with Stockfish at depth 18 and a refutation-depth ladder on every mistake of ours.

Three patterns came out of the annotation and each turned into a change:

  • Losses were middlegame slides around our own king. Every loss against a strong opponent had the same shape: a pawn grab or pawn push that opened lines toward our king, scored as fine by the net and refuted by Stockfish at shallow depth. That is evaluation knowledge, specifically king safety, and it is the reason the next network needs different data rather than a different schedule.
  • Won endgames stalled. One game reached halfmove 96, four plies from a fifty-move draw, in a won rook ending. The cause was measurable: the net scored keeping a pawn on b2 at +20 pawns and the position after promoting and trading at +16.5, so the search never promoted. The fifty-move fade and mop-up term fixed the demonstrated position, and the bounded check extension let rook endings search deeper than 7 to 10 plies.
  • The clock was under-used by about 25%. Both builds finished games with 23 seconds unused, and the mistakes were made on moves given 1.5 seconds against a mean of 2.1. The move-number horizon spends that time in the middlegame where the errors were.

One platform rule was only visible from the games: it ends a game as soon as the side to move could claim a threefold repetition. My engine recorded positions it was asked about but not the positions its own moves created, so it shuffled through them until a claim opened on its own turn. Six rated draws from winning positions, fixed with one method that records the position after our move.

9A transformer spike, and why it stayed a spike

I measured whether a Chessformer-style transformer could play any role under the constraints. On one core with torch 2.13 and onnxruntime 1.29, a 1.9 million parameter model (d128, 4 layers) evaluates 250 to 320 positions a second; the paper's 6 million parameter class manages 75 to 90. The alpha-beta search evaluates 300,000. A transformer therefore cannot be the leaf evaluation; it is 1,000 times too slow. On the training side the GTX 1660 manages 5,200 samples a second for the smallest model, which is about 450 million samples a day against the 5 billion the paper's smallest model used on an A100.

The one shape that fits is a policy prior at the root and first ply, ordering moves for the search at a few percent of the move budget. That is a real idea for a later version, but the numba rewrite was the surer and larger win, so the transformer stayed on its own branch.

12What I would do next

  1. Data, not schedule. Build a training set heavy in king-attack middlegames and pawn endings, including the positions from our own lost games, labelled by deep search, and fine-tune net2 on it. Every diagnosis in sections 4 and 8 points here.
  2. The rest of the Stockfish search list, each through the gate: razoring, ProbCut, correction history, continuation history, a shallower TT cutoff at non-PV nodes, negative extensions.
  3. Speed. Move scoring costs 0.67 µs a call and about 1.4 µs a node is unaccounted overhead in the kernel; both are targets before the evaluation is.
  4. A policy prior at the root from the small transformer, capped at a few percent of the move budget.
